## The 7 crystal systems

### Popover
helpCrystalSystemsTitle: The 7 crystal systems
helpCrystalSystemsExplain: A crystal system tells you the shape of the unit cell: which edge lengths are equal and which angles are 90° or 120°. The shape comes from the rotation symmetry of the lattice, and there are exactly seven possibilities, from cubic (most symmetric) to triclinic (no rules at all).
helpCrystalSystemsFormula1Tag: Your lattice: {label}
helpCrystalSystemsNow: {label}: {sYSTEMBLURBS}

### SEE MORE: Derivation
[diagram: drawn at the top of the derivation]
helpCrystalSystemsStep1: A lattice is fixed by six numbers: three edge lengths and three angles.
helpCrystalSystemsStep1Tex: a,\ b,\ c,\qquad \alpha = \angle(\mathbf b,\mathbf c),\quad \beta = \angle(\mathbf a,\mathbf c),\quad \gamma = \angle(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
helpCrystalSystemsStep2: A rotation that maps the lattice onto itself turns a lattice vector $\mathbf t$ into another lattice vector. Rotating $\mathbf t$ by $+\varphi$ and by $-\varphi$ and adding the results gives a vector along $\mathbf t$, which must again be a whole multiple of $\mathbf t$.
helpCrystalSystemsStep2Tex: R_{+\varphi}\,\mathbf t + R_{-\varphi}\,\mathbf t = 2\cos\varphi\;\mathbf t = m\,\mathbf t,\qquad m \in \mathbb Z
helpCrystalSystemsStep3: So $2\cos\varphi$ can only be −2, −1, 0, 1 or 2. A lattice can only have 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotation axes: no 5-fold or 7-fold ones.
helpCrystalSystemsStep3Tex: 2\cos\varphi \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\ \Rightarrow\ \varphi = 180^\circ,\ 120^\circ,\ 90^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 360^\circ
helpCrystalSystemsStep4: Each system is defined by the rotation axes it must have, and every axis forces some lengths or angles to be equal:
helpCrystalSystemsStep4Tex: \small\begin{array}{lll} \text{triclinic} & \text{no axis needed} & \text{no rules}\\ \text{monoclinic} & \text{one 2-fold} \parallel \mathbf b & \alpha=\gamma=90^\circ\\ \text{orthorhombic} & \text{three 2-fold} & \alpha=\beta=\gamma=90^\circ\\ \text{tetragonal} & \text{one 4-fold} \parallel \mathbf c & a=b,\ \text{all }90^\circ\\ \text{rhombohedral} & \text{one 3-fold} & a=b=c,\ \alpha=\beta=\gamma\\ \text{hexagonal} & \text{one 6-fold} \parallel \mathbf c & a=b,\ \gamma=120^\circ\\ \text{cubic} & \text{four 3-fold} & a=b=c,\ \text{all }90^\circ \end{array}
helpCrystalSystemsStep5: The four 3-fold axes of the cubic system lie along the body diagonals: turning the cube by 120° about a body diagonal swaps a → b → c, so the three edges must be equal.
helpCrystalSystemsStep6: Strictly speaking these seven are the <b>lattice systems</b>. Books that count <b>crystal systems</b> use “trigonal” (any 3-fold axis) instead of “rhombohedral”; a trigonal crystal can have a rhombohedral or a hexagonal lattice.
helpCrystalSystemsStep7: For a bare lattice the cell shape decides the symmetry. In a real crystal the atoms inside the cell decide, so a crystal can be tetragonal even when c happens to be very close to a.

## Centring types P, I, F, C (and R)

### Popover
helpCentringTitle: Centring types P, I, F, C (and R)
helpCentringExplain: Centring adds extra lattice points to the cell: I (body-centred) one in the middle, F (face-centred) one on every face, C (base-centred) one on the two ab faces, while P (primitive) has points on the corners only. Every point still has identical surroundings, so it is still a lattice. A centred cell is used because it shows the symmetry better than the smaller primitive cell.
helpCentringFormula1Tag: Centring vectors
helpCentringFormula1: \mathbf t_I = \tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c),\quad \mathbf t_C = \tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b),\quad \mathbf t_F = \tfrac12(\mathbf b+\mathbf c),\ \tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf c),\ \tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b)
helpCentringFormula2Tag: Points per cell
helpCentringFormula2: N_P = 1,\quad N_I = 2,\quad N_C = 2,\quad N_F = 4,\quad N_R = 3\ \text{(hexagonal axes)}
helpCentringSym1: \mathbf t
helpCentringSym1Text: a centring vector: adding it to any lattice point gives another lattice point
helpCentringSym2: N
helpCentringSym2Text: lattice points per conventional cell
helpCentringSym3: \mathbf a,\ \mathbf b,\ \mathbf c
helpCentringSym3Text: the lattice vectors (cell edges)
helpCentringNow: {lattice}: {centring} ({letter}), {total} points per cell.
helpCentringNowOne: {lattice}: {centring} ({letter}), {total} point per cell.

[3D highlight: the "Show in the 3D view" box, above the tabs]

### SEE MORE: Derivation
[diagram: drawn at the top of the derivation]
helpCentringStep1: A centring vector $\mathbf t$ adds a lattice point at $\mathbf r + \mathbf t$ for every lattice point $\mathbf r$ of the primitive pattern.
helpCentringStep1Tex: \mathbf r' = \mathbf r + \mathbf t
helpCentringStep2: The result must still be a lattice, so adding $\mathbf t$ twice must land on a cell corner: $2\mathbf t$ has whole-number components. Each component of $\mathbf t$ is therefore 0 or ½.
helpCentringStep2Tex: 2\mathbf t \in \mathbb Z^3\ \Rightarrow\ t_i \in \{0,\ \tfrac12\}
helpCentringStep3: That leaves a body vector (I) or a face vector (C, or A and B for the other faces, which are the same lattice with relabelled axes).
helpCentringStep3Tex: (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12) \to I,\qquad (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0) \to C
helpCentringStep4: Two different face centrings force the third: their sum is the third face vector plus a whole cell edge. So exactly two centred face pairs is impossible, and all three together give F.
helpCentringStep4Tex: (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0) + (0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12) = (\tfrac12,1,\tfrac12) \equiv (\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)
helpCentringStep5: Counting: the 8 corners give 1 point per cell, and every centring vector adds one more.
helpCentringStep5Tex: N_I = 1 + 1 = 2,\qquad N_C = 1 + 1 = 2,\qquad N_F = 1 + 3 = 4
helpCentringStep6: R is special: the rhombohedral lattice drawn on hexagonal axes has extra points at (⅔, ⅓, ⅓) and (⅓, ⅔, ⅔), so 3 per hexagonal cell. On rhombohedral axes the same lattice is primitive.
helpCentringStep6Tex: \mathbf t_R = (\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac13),\ (\tfrac13,\tfrac23,\tfrac23)\ \Rightarrow\ N_R = 3
helpCentringStep7: Every centred lattice also has a primitive cell, N times smaller. For cubic F it is a rhombohedron with edges a/√2 at 60°: correct, but it hides the cubic symmetry, which is why the cube is used.
helpCentringStep7Tex: V_\text{prim} = \frac{V}{N}

## Why exactly 14 Bravais lattices?

### Popover
helpBravaisTitle: Why exactly 14 Bravais lattices?
helpBravaisExplain: Combining the 7 crystal systems with the centring types P, C, I and F gives 28 candidates, but only 14 are really different lattices. The others are either a lattice that is already on the list, drawn with an unnecessarily large cell, or they break the symmetry that defines their system.
helpBravaisFormula1: \underbrace{3}_{\text{cubic}} + \underbrace{2}_{\text{tetr.}} + \underbrace{4}_{\text{orth.}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{hex.}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{rhomb.}} + \underbrace{2}_{\text{mono.}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{tricl.}} = 14
helpBravaisSym1: P,\ C,\ I,\ F
helpBravaisSym1Text: primitive, base-centred, body-centred, face-centred
helpBravaisSym2: R
helpBravaisSym2Text: the rhombohedral lattice (its primitive cell)
helpBravaisNow: {name} is one of the {count} {system} lattices.

### SEE MORE: Derivation
[diagram: drawn at the top of the derivation]
helpBravaisStep1: Start with every combination of a crystal system with a centring type.
helpBravaisStep1Tex: 7\ \text{systems} \times \{P,\ C,\ I,\ F\} = 28\ \text{candidates}
helpBravaisStep2: <b>Rule 1: the same lattice in disguise.</b> If a centred cell can be redrawn as a smaller cell of the same system, it is not new. Tetragonal C: new axes along the diagonals of the square base give a smaller square base with no centring.
helpBravaisStep2Tex: \mathbf a' = \tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b),\quad \mathbf b' = \tfrac12(\mathbf a-\mathbf b),\quad |\mathbf a'| = |\mathbf b'| = \tfrac{a}{\sqrt2}\ \Rightarrow\ tC = tP
helpBravaisStep3: The same trick shows that tetragonal F is tetragonal I, monoclinic I and F are monoclinic C, and every centred triclinic cell has a smaller primitive triclinic cell.
helpBravaisStep3Tex: tF = tI,\qquad mI = mF = mC,\qquad aC = aI = aF = aP
helpBravaisStep4: <b>Rule 2: symmetry broken.</b> Centring only one pair of faces of a cube treats c differently from a and b, so the 3-fold axes along the body diagonals are lost: “cubic C” is really tetragonal. Centring a hexagonal cell destroys its 6-fold axis in the same way.
helpBravaisStep4Tex: cC \to \text{tetragonal},\qquad hC,\ hI,\ hF \to \text{lower symmetry}
helpBravaisStep5: Orthorhombic is the exception: a, b and c are all different and no symmetry links them, so no smaller orthorhombic cell exists for any centring. All four survive.
helpBravaisStep5Tex: oP,\quad oC,\quad oI,\quad oF
helpBravaisStep6: Count what is left:
helpBravaisStep6Tex: cP,\,cI,\,cF \;\cdot\; tP,\,tI \;\cdot\; oP,\,oC,\,oI,\,oF \;\cdot\; hP \;\cdot\; hR \;\cdot\; mP,\,mC \;\cdot\; aP\ =\ 14

## Lattice vectors and parameters

### Popover
helpLatticeParamsTitle: Lattice vectors and parameters
helpLatticeParamsExplain: Three lattice vectors a, b and c start at the origin and span the unit cell. Their lengths a, b, c and the angles α (between b and c), β (between a and c) and γ (between a and b) are the six lattice parameters. Every lattice point is reached by whole steps along the three vectors.
helpLatticeParamsFormula1: \mathbf r = n_1\mathbf a + n_2\mathbf b + n_3\mathbf c,\qquad n_1, n_2, n_3 \in \mathbb Z
helpLatticeParamsSym1: \mathbf a,\ \mathbf b,\ \mathbf c
helpLatticeParamsSym1Text: the lattice vectors (bold: vectors)
helpLatticeParamsSym3: n_1, n_2, n_3
helpLatticeParamsSym3Text: whole numbers: how many steps along each vector
helpLatticeParamsNow: On screen: ${paramsTex}$

[3D highlight: the "Show in the 3D view" box, above the tabs]

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpLatticeParamsStep1: Pick a lattice point as the origin. The vectors to three neighbouring lattice points that do not lie in one plane are the lattice vectors.
helpLatticeParamsStep1Tex: \mathbf a,\ \mathbf b,\ \mathbf c
helpLatticeParamsStep2: Their lengths and the angles between them are the lattice parameters:
helpLatticeParamsStep2Tex: a = |\mathbf a|,\qquad \cos\alpha = \frac{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c}{bc},\quad \cos\beta = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c}{ac},\quad \cos\gamma = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{ab}
helpLatticeParamsStep3: To draw the cell, the app puts $\mathbf a$ along x and $\mathbf b$ in the xy-plane:
helpLatticeParamsStep3Tex: \mathbf a = (a,\ 0,\ 0),\qquad \mathbf b = (b\cos\gamma,\ b\sin\gamma,\ 0)
helpLatticeParamsStep4: The components of $\mathbf c$ follow from its angles with $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$; its height makes the cell volume come out right:
helpLatticeParamsStep4Tex: \mathbf c = \Big(c\cos\beta,\ \ c\,\frac{\cos\alpha - \cos\beta\cos\gamma}{\sin\gamma},\ \ \frac{V}{ab\sin\gamma}\Big)
helpLatticeParamsStep5: Positions are written as fractional coordinates (x, y, z) in units of the cell edges. Whole numbers are lattice points of a primitive lattice; centred lattices add the centring vectors.
helpLatticeParamsStep5Tex: \mathbf r = x\,\mathbf a + y\,\mathbf b + z\,\mathbf c
