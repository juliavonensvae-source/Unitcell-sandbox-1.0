## Ionic structures: lattice + basis

### Popover
helpIonicTitle: Ionic structures: lattice + basis
helpIonicScope: Rock salt, CsCl, zinc blende, fluorite
helpIonicExplain: A crystal structure is a Bravais lattice with the same group of atoms, the <b>basis</b>, at every lattice point. In an ionic crystal the basis holds the ions of one formula unit, so rock salt is cubic F with the basis Cl⁻ + Na⁺, not a lattice of its own. The ions per cell, the formula units Z and the coordination numbers all follow from the lattice and the basis.
helpIonicFormula2: N_X = N_\text{lattice}\times n_X,\qquad Z = \frac{N_X}{\text{(number of }X\text{ in the formula)}}
helpIonicFormula3Tag: Radius-ratio rule
helpIonicFormula3: \frac{r_+}{r_-} \ge 0.732 \Rightarrow 8,\quad \ge 0.414 \Rightarrow 6,\quad \ge 0.225 \Rightarrow 4
helpIonicSym1: n_X
helpIonicSym1Text: number of ions X in the basis
helpIonicSym2: N_\text{lattice}
helpIonicSym2Text: lattice points per cell
helpIonicNow: {formula}: {name} + {basis} → {ions} per cell, Z = {z}.

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpIonicStep1: A Bravais lattice is a set of translations: shifting the crystal by one of them maps it onto itself. In a crystal of two kinds of ions, a shift that takes a cation onto an anion is not one of them.
helpIonicStep2: So the lattice is found from one kind of ion alone, and everything else is the basis: the ions you place at every lattice point, given in fractions of a, b, c.
helpIonicStep2Tex: \mathbf r = \mathbf T + \mathbf r_j,\qquad \mathbf T = n_1\mathbf a + n_2\mathbf b + n_3\mathbf c
helpIonicStep3: Each lattice point carries one copy of the basis, so the ions of kind X per cell are:
helpIonicStep3Tex: N_X = N_\text{lattice}\times n_X
helpIonicStep4: Counted in the drawn cell with shares, the same numbers come out. Rock salt (Na⁺ on the edges and in the centre, Cl⁻ on corners and faces):
helpIonicStep4Tex: N_{\mathrm{Na^+}} = 12\cdot\tfrac14 + 1 = 4,\qquad N_{\mathrm{Cl^-}} = 8\cdot\tfrac18 + 6\cdot\tfrac12 = 4
helpIonicStep5: The basis is one formula unit (or a few), so the formula units per cell are:
helpIonicStep5Tex: Z_\mathrm{NaCl} = 4,\quad Z_\mathrm{CsCl} = 1,\quad Z_\mathrm{ZnS} = 4,\quad Z_{\mathrm{CaF_2}} = 4
helpIonicStep6: The coordination number is how many ions of the other kind are nearest. A cation stays in contact with all its anions only if it is big enough for the hole they make; the critical ratios are those of the interstitial sites:
helpIonicStep6Tex: \text{cube } 0.732,\qquad \text{octahedron } 0.414,\qquad \text{tetrahedron } 0.225
helpIonicStep7: So r₊/r₋ predicts the structure: CsCl (0.96) CN 8, NaCl (0.56) CN 6, ZnS (0.33) CN 4. The rule is a guide only: real ions are not hard spheres, and partly covalent bonds (as in ZnS) bend it.

## NaCl (rock salt)
helpIonicExplainNaCl: Rock salt is <b>cubic F with a basis of two ions</b>: Cl⁻ at (0, 0, 0) and Na⁺ at (½, ½, ½). The Cl⁻ ions form a face-centred cubic array and the Na⁺ ions fill every octahedral hole in it, so each ion has 6 neighbours of the other kind. It is not a simple cubic lattice: neighbouring positions hold different ions.
helpIonicPointNaCl: All 8 ions of a small cube of edge a/2 look like simple cubic, but next-door ions are different. The translations that map the whole crystal onto itself are those of cubic F, so the lattice is cubic F and the second ion belongs to the basis.

## CsCl (caesium chloride)
helpIonicExplainCsCl: CsCl is <b>cubic P with a basis of two ions</b>: Cl⁻ at the corner (0, 0, 0) and Cs⁺ at the body centre (½, ½, ½). It looks like body-centred cubic, but it is not: the corner and the centre hold different ions, so (½, ½, ½) is not a lattice translation. Each ion has 8 neighbours of the other kind.
helpIonicPointCsCl: A common mistake is to call CsCl body-centred cubic. Shifting the crystal by (½, ½, ½) moves Cl⁻ onto Cs⁺, so that shift is not a lattice translation. Only whole cell edges are: the lattice is cubic P, with 1 lattice point and 1 formula unit per cell.

## ZnS (zinc blende)
helpIonicExplainZnS: Zinc blende is <b>cubic F with a basis of two ions</b>: S²⁻ at (0, 0, 0) and Zn²⁺ at (¼, ¼, ¼). The S²⁻ ions form a face-centred cubic array and Zn²⁺ fills half of its tetrahedral holes, so each ion has 4 neighbours of the other kind. With all atoms the same it would be the diamond structure.
helpIonicPointZnS: Cubic F has 8 tetrahedral holes per cell; Zn²⁺ takes the 4 at (¼, ¼, ¼) + the centring vectors and leaves the 4 at (¾, ¾, ¾)-type positions empty. Replace both ions by C or Si and you get diamond.

## CaF₂ (fluorite)
helpIonicExplainCaF2: Fluorite is <b>cubic F with a basis of three ions</b>: Ca²⁺ at (0, 0, 0) and F⁻ at (¼, ¼, ¼) and (¾, ¾, ¾). The Ca²⁺ ions form a face-centred cubic array and F⁻ fills every tetrahedral hole, so there are twice as many F⁻ as Ca²⁺. Each Ca²⁺ has 8 F⁻ around it and each F⁻ has 4 Ca²⁺.
helpIonicPointCaF2: The coordination numbers follow the formula: each Ca²⁺ has 8 F⁻ and each F⁻ has 4 Ca²⁺, and 8 : 4 = 2 : 1, as in CaF₂. Seen from the F⁻ ions, they form a simple cubic array with a Ca²⁺ in every second cube centre.
