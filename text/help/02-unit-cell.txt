## Points or touching spheres

### Popover
helpDisplayModeTitle: Points or touching spheres
helpDisplayModeExplain: “Points” marks where the lattice points are. “Touching spheres” makes each point a sphere just big enough to touch its nearest neighbours, so the radius is half the nearest-neighbour distance. This hard-sphere model is the one used for packing fractions and interstitial holes.
helpDisplayModeFormula1: r = \frac{d_\text{nn}}{2}
helpDisplayModeSym1: r

## The unit cell

### Popover
helpCellEdgesTitle: The unit cell
helpCellEdgesExplain: The unit cell is the parallelepiped spanned by a, b and c. Copies of it, shifted by whole lattice vectors, fill all of space without gaps or overlaps. In a supercell, “Show the edges of every cell” draws the lines between the lattice points so you can see each copy.
helpCellEdgesFormula1: V = \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)

## Cell volume

### Popover
helpVolumeScope: General formula: all lattices
helpVolumeExplain: The volume of the unit cell is its base area times its height, which is the scalar triple product of the three lattice vectors. Written with the lattice parameters it becomes one general formula that works for every crystal system and simplifies a lot when the angles are 90°.
helpVolumeFormula2Tag: {system} only
# The formula itself comes from 00-shared.txt (formulaVol…), except hexagonal:
helpVolumeHexagonal: V = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,a^2c
helpVolumeSym2: V
helpVolumeSym2Text: cell volume (Å³)
helpVolumeNow: On screen: $V = {volume}\,\text{Å}^3$

[3D highlight: the "Show in the 3D view" box, above the tabs]

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpVolumeStep1: The volume of a parallelepiped is the area of its base times its height. The cross product $\mathbf b \times \mathbf c$ has the base area as its length and points straight out of the base, so a dot product with $\mathbf a$ picks out the height:
helpVolumeStep1Tex: V = \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) = \det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf a & \mathbf b & \mathbf c\end{pmatrix}
helpVolumeStep2: Put the three vectors as columns of a matrix A. Then the matrix of all dot products, the metric tensor G, is AᵀA, so its determinant is V².
helpVolumeStep2Tex: G = A^\mathsf T A\ \Rightarrow\ \det G = (\det A)^2 = V^2
helpVolumeStep3: Write G with the lattice parameters ($\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = ab\cos\gamma$ and so on):
helpVolumeStep3Tex: G = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab\cos\gamma & ac\cos\beta \\ ab\cos\gamma & b^2 & bc\cos\alpha \\ ac\cos\beta & bc\cos\alpha & c^2 \end{pmatrix}
helpVolumeStep4: Expanding the determinant and taking the square root gives the general formula:
helpVolumeStep4Tex: V^2 = a^2b^2c^2\,(1 - \cos^2\alpha - \cos^2\beta - \cos^2\gamma + 2\cos\alpha\cos\beta\cos\gamma)
helpVolumeStep5: With right angles every cosine is 0. Hexagonal has γ = 120°, monoclinic only β ≠ 90°:
helpVolumeStep5Tex: \text{cubic } a^3,\quad \text{tetragonal } a^2c,\quad \text{orthorhombic } abc,\quad \text{hexagonal } \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2c,\quad \text{monoclinic } abc\sin\beta
helpVolumeStep6: Rhombohedral: all three angles equal α.

### SEE MORE: Your lattice (worked example)
helpVolumeEx1: The parameters on screen:
helpVolumeEx2: The cosines of the angles:
helpVolumeEx2Tex: \cos\alpha = {ca},\quad \cos\beta = {cb},\quad \cos\gamma = {cg}
helpVolumeEx3: The bracket under the square root:
helpVolumeEx3Tex: 1 - ({ca})^2 - ({cb})^2 - ({cg})^2 + 2({ca})({cb})({cg}) = {root}
helpVolumeEx4: Multiply by abc:
helpVolumeEx4Tex: V = {a}\times{b}\times{c}\times\sqrt{{root}} = {volume}\,\text{Å}^3
helpVolumeEx5: The hexagonal prism on screen is three of these cells:
helpVolumeEx5Tex: 3V = {prism}\,\text{Å}^3


## Lattice points per unit cell

### Popover
helpPointsTitle: Lattice points per unit cell
helpPointsExplain: A point inside the cell belongs to it alone, but a point on a face is shared with the neighbouring cell, a point on an edge with 4 cells and a point on a corner with 8. Adding up the shares gives the number of lattice points per cell: 1 for P, 2 for I and C, 4 for F.
helpPointsFormula1: N = N_\text{inside} + \tfrac12 N_\text{face} + \tfrac14 N_\text{edge} + \tfrac18 N_\text{corner}
helpPointsFormula2: N = \tfrac16 N_\text{corner} + \tfrac12 N_\text{face} = 12\times\tfrac16 + 2\times\tfrac12 = 3
helpPointsSym1: N
helpPointsSym1Text: lattice points per cell
helpPointsSym2: N_\text{corner},\ N_\text{edge},\ \ldots
helpPointsSym2Text: how many points sit on the corners, edges, faces or inside
helpPointsNow: On screen: ${count} = {total}$

[3D highlight: the "Show in the 3D view" box, above the tabs]

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpPointsStep1: Count how many cells meet at each kind of position. Around a corner there are 8 cells (2 × 2 × 2), along an edge 4, across a face 2. This is true for every parallelepiped, also for slanted cells.
helpPointsStep1Tex: \text{corner: } \tfrac18,\qquad \text{edge: } \tfrac14,\qquad \text{face: } \tfrac12,\qquad \text{inside: } 1
helpPointsStep2: Add up the shares of all points of one cell:
helpPointsStep3: The four centring types:
helpPointsStep3Tex: P:\ 8\times\tfrac18 = 1\qquad I:\ 8\times\tfrac18 + 1 = 2\qquad C:\ 8\times\tfrac18 + 2\times\tfrac12 = 2\qquad F:\ 8\times\tfrac18 + 6\times\tfrac12 = 4
helpPointsStep4: In a slanted cell the 8 corner pieces have different shapes, but moved to one corner they fit together into exactly one whole sphere (the 8 cells around a point fill all the space around it). So the counting still works.
helpPointsStep5: The hexagonal prism is different: 3 prisms meet at each vertical edge, so a corner is shared by 3 × 2 = 6 prisms. With 12 corners and 2 face centres:
helpPointsStep5Tex: N = 12\times\tfrac16 + 2\times\tfrac12 = 3
helpPointsStep6: N also tells you how many times larger the cell is than a primitive cell:
helpPointsStep6Tex: N = \frac{V_\text{cell}}{V_\text{prim}}

## Metric tensor G

### Popover
helpMetricExplain: The metric tensor G collects all dot products of the lattice vectors. With it, lengths, angles and volumes can be worked out straight from the indices, in any crystal system, without Cartesian coordinates. Its inverse G* does the same for the reciprocal lattice, which gives plane spacings and angles between planes.
helpMetricFormula1: G_{ij} = \mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf a_j = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab\cos\gamma & ac\cos\beta \\ ab\cos\gamma & b^2 & bc\cos\alpha \\ ac\cos\beta & bc\cos\alpha & c^2 \end{pmatrix}
helpMetricFormula2: G^* = G^{-1},\qquad \det G = V^2
helpMetricSym1: \mathbf a_1, \mathbf a_2, \mathbf a_3
helpMetricSym1Text: the lattice vectors a, b, c
helpMetricSym2: G^*
helpMetricSym2Text: the reciprocal metric tensor
helpMetricSym3: V
helpMetricSym3Text: cell volume

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpMetricStep1: Write a vector with its components: $\mathbf r = \sum_i u_i\mathbf a_i$. The dot product of two such vectors only needs the dot products of the axes:
helpMetricStep1Tex: \mathbf r_1\cdot\mathbf r_2 = \sum_{i,j} u_{1i}\,u_{2j}\,(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf a_j) = \mathbf u_1^\mathsf T G\,\mathbf u_2
helpMetricStep2: So lengths and angles of directions follow directly:
helpMetricStep2Tex: |\mathbf r| = \sqrt{\mathbf u^\mathsf T G\,\mathbf u},\qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf u_1^\mathsf T G\,\mathbf u_2}{|\mathbf r_1|\,|\mathbf r_2|}
helpMetricStep3: The reciprocal vectors $\mathbf a_i^*$ are defined by $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf a_j^* = \delta_{ij}$ (1 if i = j, else 0). Writing $\mathbf a_i^* = \sum_k X_{ik}\mathbf a_k$ gives XG = 1, so X = G⁻¹, and their metric tensor is G* = G⁻¹:
helpMetricStep3Tex: \mathbf a_i^* = \sum_k (G^{-1})_{ik}\,\mathbf a_k\ \Rightarrow\ G^*_{ij} = \mathbf a_i^*\cdot\mathbf a_j^* = (G^{-1})_{ij}
helpMetricStep4: G* gives plane normals g = h a* + k b* + l c*, whose length is 1/d:
helpMetricStep4Tex: \frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \mathbf h^\mathsf T G^*\,\mathbf h
helpMetricStep5: And the determinant is the squared volume, because G = AᵀA with the lattice vectors as the columns of A:
helpMetricStep5Tex: \det G = (\det A)^2 = V^2

### SEE MORE: Your lattice (worked example)
helpMetricEx1: G from the parameters on screen (Å²):
helpMetricEx1Tex: G = {matrix}
helpMetricEx2: Its inverse (Å⁻²):
helpMetricEx2Tex: G^* = G^{-1} = {matrix}
helpMetricEx3: The determinant and the volume:
helpMetricEx3Tex: \det G = {detG}\,\text{Å}^6\ \Rightarrow\ V = \sqrt{\det G} = {volume}\,\text{Å}^3
helpMetricEx4: Example: the length of [110] = a + b.
helpMetricEx4Tex: |\mathbf a + \mathbf b| = \sqrt{G_{11} + 2G_{12} + G_{22}} = \sqrt{{g11} + 2({g12}) + {g22}} = {length}\,\text{Å}


## Hexagonal prism

### Popover
helpHexPrismScope: Hexagonal P
helpHexPrismExplain: Three primitive hexagonal cells, rhombi with γ = 120°, fit together into a hexagonal prism that shows the 6-fold symmetry at a glance. It has 12 corner points plus one point in the centre of the top and bottom faces, and holds 3 lattice points.
helpHexPrismFormula1: N = 12\times\tfrac16 + 2\times\tfrac12 = 3
helpHexPrismFormula2: V_\text{prism} = 3V = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\,a^2c
helpHexPrismSym1: a,\ c
helpHexPrismSym1Text: hexagonal lattice parameters
helpHexPrismSym2: V
helpHexPrismSym2Text: volume of one primitive cell

[3D highlight: the "Show in the 3D view" box, above the tabs]
helpHexPrismHighlight: Choose <b>Hexagonal P</b> and <b>Hexagonal prism</b> in the <b>Add cells</b> drawer (right edge) to see the shares highlighted.

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpHexPrismStep1: The base of the primitive cell is a rhombus with sides a and an angle of 120°:
helpHexPrismStep1Tex: A_\text{prim} = a^2\sin120^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,a^2
helpHexPrismStep2: Three rhombi around a common corner fill a full turn, because each has 120° there. Together they form a regular hexagon.
helpHexPrismStep2Tex: 3\times120^\circ = 360^\circ
helpHexPrismStep3: Hexagonal prisms tile space like a honeycomb: 3 prisms meet at each vertical edge, and there are prisms above and below. A corner is shared by 6 prisms.
helpHexPrismStep3Tex: \text{corner share} = \frac{1}{3\times2} = \frac16
helpHexPrismStep4: The centres of the top and bottom faces are shared by 2 prisms each. Adding up:
helpHexPrismStep4Tex: N = 12\times\tfrac16 + 2\times\tfrac12 = 2 + 1 = 3
helpHexPrismStep5: Three cells, so three times the volume:
helpHexPrismStep5Tex: V_\text{prism} = 3\times\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2c = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}a^2c

## Rhombohedral or hexagonal axes

### Popover
helpRhombohedralSettingTitle: Rhombohedral or hexagonal axes
helpRhombohedralSettingScope: Rhombohedral R
helpRhombohedralSettingExplain: The rhombohedral lattice can be drawn with its primitive cell (three equal edges a_R at equal angles α, 1 point per cell) or with a hexagonal cell three times larger that has extra points at (⅔, ⅓, ⅓) and (⅓, ⅔, ⅔). Both describe exactly the same lattice points.
helpRhombohedralSettingFormula1: a_H = 2a_R\sin\frac{\alpha}{2},\qquad c_H = a_R\sqrt{3(1+2\cos\alpha)}
helpRhombohedralSettingFormula2: V_H = 3V_R
helpRhombohedralSettingSym1: a_R,\ \alpha
helpRhombohedralSettingSym1Text: rhombohedral parameters
helpRhombohedralSettingSym2: a_H,\ c_H
helpRhombohedralSettingSym2Text: hexagonal parameters of the same lattice

### SEE MORE: Derivation
helpRhombohedralSettingStep1: The 3-fold axis of the rhombohedron runs along the sum of its three equal edge vectors. That sum is the hexagonal c axis.
helpRhombohedralSettingStep1Tex: \mathbf c_H = \mathbf a_R + \mathbf b_R + \mathbf c_R
helpRhombohedralSettingStep2: Its length, using $\mathbf a_R\cdot\mathbf b_R = a_R^2\cos\alpha$ for each of the 3 pairs (counted twice):
helpRhombohedralSettingStep2Tex: c_H^2 = 3a_R^2 + 6a_R^2\cos\alpha = 3a_R^2(1 + 2\cos\alpha)
helpRhombohedralSettingStep3: A hexagonal a axis is the difference of two rhombohedral vectors. It is perpendicular to $\mathbf c_H$:
helpRhombohedralSettingStep3Tex: \mathbf a_H = \mathbf a_R - \mathbf b_R,\qquad a_H^2 = 2a_R^2(1-\cos\alpha) = 4a_R^2\sin^2\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
helpRhombohedralSettingStep4: The hexagonal cell contains 3 lattice points, at (0, 0, 0), (⅔, ⅓, ⅓) and (⅓, ⅔, ⅔), so it is 3 times larger than the primitive cell.
helpRhombohedralSettingStep4Tex: V_H = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,a_H^2c_H = 3V_R
